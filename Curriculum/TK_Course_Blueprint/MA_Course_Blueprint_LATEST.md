1\. The Spine of the Programme

The programme is not framed as M&A training. It is framed as a
leadership journey that begins with invention and ends with
institutional renewal. M&A is one move inside that journey --- the one a
firm makes when the capability it needs already lives inside another
company. The whole architecture follows a single progression:

> **Invention → Innovation → Capability → Capital Allocation → Build /
> Buy / Partner / Acquire → Integration → Institutional Renewal**

**The message to every leader:** *"M&A is not the starting point.
Strategic ambition is the starting point. M&A becomes relevant only when
the firm lacks the capability, technology, talent, IP, access or speed
required to achieve that ambition."*

The three layers and the shift each one makes

The same firm sends people to all three layers, three times a year, for
several years. A promoter, a senior leader and a functional manager from
one company travel the same journey at their own altitude --- which is
what makes this a whole-firm capability build, not a course.

2\. The Opener --- "The Engine We Couldn't Build, and the Rail We Did"

*The intellectual and emotional provocation that opens The Council and
The Watch. Told version runs 6--7 minutes. Wright-brothers history sets
perspective; aircraft engines, pharma and semiconductors widen the lens;
the two Indian engines are the emotional core; UPI is the hopeful
close.*

> Begin a century back. In 1903, two brothers who ran a bicycle shop in
> Dayton decided to fly. They had the wings. They had the controls. What
> they didn't have was an engine light enough to leave the ground ---
> and when they wrote to manufacturer after manufacturer, every one said
> no. So they turned to the mechanic in their shop, Charlie Taylor, and
> he built the engine by hand. On 17 December, the machine flew --- 120
> feet, twelve seconds. Twelve seconds that opened a 120-year journey.
>
> I start there for one reason. The brothers didn't buy flight. They
> built the capability for it, piece by piece --- including the one
> piece nobody would sell them: the engine.
>
> Now run the clock forward. That same component became the single
> hardest thing in all of industry to make. Today the world has two
> serious commercial planemakers --- and effectively four companies that
> build the engines. The airframe opened up; the engine stayed closed. A
> bicycle mechanic built the first one in six weeks; no startup on earth
> can build a modern one. That gap is the entire story of deep
> capability.
>
> And India has lived both sides of that gap. In the last forty years,
> we built two engines. One flew. One still hasn't.
>
> The one that flew: in 1992, when we tried to buy cryogenic
> rocket-engine technology, the United States leaned on Russia and the
> deal was killed. We were denied. Here is the detail that matters most
> --- Russia still handed us a few finished engines, and we still
> couldn't build our own. We had the object in our hands, the patents in
> our libraries, the physics in our textbooks --- and it wasn't enough.
> It took us twenty-two years of failing forward to fly an indigenous
> cryogenic engine, in 2014. Today we launch our own heavy satellites
> and we reached Mars on our first attempt, at a fraction of anyone
> else's cost.
>
> The one that hasn't: the Kaveri jet engine. We started it in 1986 ---
> six years before the cryogenic denial. Forty years and thousands of
> crores later, it still can't reliably push a fighter into the sky, and
> the Tejas flies on an American engine.
>
> Same country. Same talent. Why did one fly and not the other? Two
> reasons --- both leadership, not technology. First, the fallback.
> After the denial, ISRO had no Plan B; build it or fail. The Kaveri
> team always had an open door --- if the engine slipped, buy the GE.
> And that open door quietly killed the urgency. When buying is always
> available, the will to build never fully ignites. Second, how leaders
> held failure. In 1979, a young director named Abdul Kalam watched his
> first rocket crash into the sea --- and his chairman, Satish Dhawan,
> stood at the press conference and took the blame himself. The next
> year they succeeded, and Dhawan put Kalam in front to take the credit.
> A team protected when it fails will keep trying until it flies. That
> is the culture that builds an engine over twenty-two years.
>
> And the deepest point, the one for this room: the artifact is not the
> capability. The patent is not the capability. Capability has to be
> incubated, nurtured, failed-at, and scaled --- and it cannot be
> imported.
>
> Once you see it, you see it everywhere. We are the pharmacy of the
> world --- and we import most of the molecules that go into the
> medicine. We assemble the planet's electronics --- and import nearly
> every chip inside them. We operate more aircraft every year --- and
> build none of the engines. It is the same sentence each time: we run
> the machine; someone else owns the engine.
>
> So here is what this programme asks every leader to answer honestly:
> What is your engine? The one deep capability you have never built
> because buying it was always easier --- and is your easy supplier
> quietly making sure you never own it?
>
> Let me end where India got it right. When we built UPI, we didn't copy
> Visa or Mastercard --- we built a public rail and gave it to everyone.
> In May 2026 alone it carried 23.2 billion transactions worth nearly
> ₹30 trillion in a single month. Close to half of all the world's
> real-time digital payments now happen in India, and the rail is
> already live in seven countries, including France. We went from "we
> couldn't build the engine" to "the world now runs on the rail we
> built." Same country. The difference, every single time, was a
> decision to build --- and leaders patient enough to see it through.
>
> That is why strategy never begins with a deal. It begins with the
> engine question. M&A is one answer --- the fastest one --- when the
> capability already lives inside someone else's company. But remember
> ISRO holding those Russian engines: buying the company is not the same
> as absorbing the capability. Do the deal and fail to absorb, and you
> are the airline again --- owning the machine, unable to build it.
> Absorbing it is a three-to-five-year act of leadership. That is
> integration. That is what we are here to build.

How each module is taught --- the inverted loop

The programme deliberately inverts the usual theory-first order. In
every module the sequence is: challenge → gap analysis → just-in-time
theory → synthesis. Participants are put into a designed challenge
before they are given the frameworks, so they discover their own gaps
--- because a gap you discover yourself creates appetite for the theory,
while a gap pointed out creates defensiveness.

**The academic spine:** the sequence draws on Kolb's experiential
learning cycle (1984) and Kapur's productive failure (2008) ---
grappling with a problem before instruction deepens learning --- and on
Knowles's adult-learning principles. The design is guided, not free
discovery: Kirschner, Sweller & Clark (2006) show minimally-guided
instruction underperforms for novices, but that the advantage of
guidance recedes precisely when learners bring high prior knowledge ---
which is exactly this audience of 8-to-30-year veterans.

**Two design rules.** The challenge is engineered backwards, so the gaps
it exposes are the ones the day's theory then fills; and the loop's
intensity tapers across the year --- disorientation front-loaded in M1,
lighter in M2, minimal in M3, where application dominates and theory
returns only as consolidation. Calibrated by level: mixed-team, deep-end
simulations suit The Watch (L2) and The Pride (L3); for The Council (L1)
the challenge is a high-stakes dilemma or a curated failure case to
dissect, so the gap surfaces in the situation --- never in a leader's
personal performance in front of peers.

M1 · Foundation --- Discover the Gap

M1 runs the inverted loop at full intensity. A mixed-team challenge ---
a real capability, build-vs-buy or integration decision under pressure
--- lets participants feel their gaps before anyone names them.
Structured introspection follows (what the situation demanded, where we
fell short, what a stronger version would have done), captured
individually and as a team. Only then is theory delivered, just-in-time,
on how capability is built --- in the person, the thinking, the
frameworks and the firm. The module closes with synthesis: each
participant drafts a personal Capability-Building Plan, anchored to
their Driver Statement, carried across M2, M3 and across years.

M2 · Design the Capability Pathway --- Patent, Partner or Acquire

M2 moves participants from capability-gap awareness to
capability-pathway design. The central question: once a strategic
capability gap is identified, how should the enterprise close it?

Participants examine the full set of routes to capability creation and
control --- internal R&D, licensing, academic collaboration and
sponsored research, joint development, strategic partnership, minority
investment, and acquisition with integration. The module deliberately
places M&A at the core of the capability agenda: patents and academic
institutions supply invention-side brainpower; acquisition supplies
control, speed, scale, talent and organisational muscle; and integration
is the spine through which an accessed or acquired capability actually
becomes part of the enterprise. The caveat that keeps faith with the
engine thesis --- control is not capability: an acquired capability
becomes real only through the 3--5 year absorption, and the binding
constraint is the firm's absorptive capacity, the same muscle the
Learning Curve trains.

A signature feature of M2 is the Patent-to-Enterprise Exchange (Section
6), where selected patents and research capabilities from IIT Roorkee
and other institutions are translated into industry-relevant opportunity
spaces. Participants do not examine patents as academic artifacts; they
assess whether a patent can become a product, process, platform,
component, venture, licensing opportunity, sponsored-research project,
partnership or acquisition-adjacent capability play.

The module culminates in a Capability Pathway Design (Section 5),
comparing build, license, collaborate, partner, invest and acquire
options against strategic control, speed, cost, uncertainty, capability
depth, integration burden and long-term enterprise value.

> *"The Patent-to-Enterprise Exchange gives us invention-side
> brainpower, but M&A remains the strategic core --- because the
> leadership question is not merely where ideas come from; it is which
> capabilities the firm must control, scale and integrate."*

M3 · Practical Hands-On --- Integrate and Apply

M3 is hands-on. Participants apply the year's frameworks to a practical,
end-to-end case closest to their own firm's reality, with theory
returning only as recap and consolidation --- no new load. For The Watch
and The Pride this is a structured integration and execution simulation;
for The Council it is the board-level Integration Leadership Room
capstone and the longitudinal review of each firm's progression. The
synthesis step revisits and updates the personal Capability-Building
Plan and the firm-level project, closing the loop the year opened.

5\. Signature Rituals & Tools

Proprietary instruments that recur across the programme. They are what
make the institute feel serious and ownable rather than generic.

The Driver --- the philosophy ritual (opens The Council)

Not an icebreaker and never a recital of achievements. The point is to
surface how a leader thinks --- because their mental models, not their
CV, shape the firm's next decade, its capability bets, and its legacy.

> **Said aloud at the start:** *"We don't want your résumé. We've read
> it. We want the operating system underneath it --- the beliefs that
> make you decide differently from the person who runs your
> competitor."*

Each leader picks three or four prompts, reflects privately for 20
minutes, then speaks for 6--8 minutes to the peer circle --- no slides,
no numbers:

- What do you believe about your business that most of your peers would
  disagree with?

- When you've made your hardest call, what were you actually optimising
  for --- underneath the spreadsheet?

- What have you unlearned in the last ten years? What did you once
  believe that you no longer do?

- What is the engine of your ambition --- the thing that would still
  drive you if the money were already solved?

- What do you want to still be true about your company in twenty years,
  when you're not in the room?

- Where do you knowingly trade short-term performance for long-term
  capability --- and where do you refuse to?

- What kind of risk lets you sleep, and what kind keeps you awake?

**The forward link:** each reflection is captured as a one-page Driver
Statement that becomes the lens for every later decision. In Modules 2
and 3 the leader's capital allocation and integration choices are held
up against it --- did you allocate capital like the person you said you
were? That is the longitudinal payoff.

The Learning Curve --- Imitate → Assimilate → Innovate

Invention rarely begins with a blank sheet. Almost every economy that
built deep capability climbed the same three rungs --- and lawful
reverse engineering is the disciplined technique that powers the first
two. It is the bridge between the patent and the capability: the patent
tells you what was done; reverse engineering is the work of figuring out
how --- and even then you must still incubate the deep know-how the
patent never contained. Use it to make invention feel achievable, while
keeping the destination clear --- the third rung: genuine invention and
owned IP.

Motivation stories

- **India's own pharmacy.** Under the Patents Act of 1970, India
  recognised only process patents for medicines --- which gave Indian
  firms the legal room to reverse-engineer patented molecules and sell
  them at a small fraction of the branded price. That built the
  "pharmacy of the world." And in making those generics, firms
  accumulated deep process know-how and trained a generation of
  scientists. When India adopted product patents in 2005 under TRIPS,
  the copying door closed --- and the industry was pushed up to the
  third rung, toward genuine innovation. The whole ladder, in one Indian
  story.

- **Korea's leap.** In the classic study Imitation to Innovation (1997),
  Linsu Kim shows Korean firms acquiring foreign technology,
  assimilating and improving it, and only then investing in their own
  R&D --- moving, in electronics, from reverse engineering to strategic
  alliance, and in semiconductors, leapfrogging to the world frontier. A
  useful reference for the Prof, and for the research angle.

- **ISRO, re-read.** The cryogenic story is itself a
  reverse-engineering-into-invention story: India studied and absorbed
  the denied and supplied technology, then built the indigenous
  capability the suppliers never handed over.

- **The cautionary rung.** The danger is stopping at imitation ---
  copying without ever climbing to assimilation and invention, and
  staying a permanent imitator. The rung you skip is the one that
  mattered.

> **The line that is not negotiable:** *this is lawful reverse
> engineering only --- expired patents, clean-room methods, and products
> you own and have the right to study. Never trade-secret theft or
> infringement of a live patent. The institute builds capability within
> the law.*

**The M&A connection:** two ways this lands in a deal. First, reverse
engineering is a build accelerator --- it can make "build" faster and
cheaper than it first appears, which can shift a Build / Buy / Partner /
Acquire decision away from "buy." Second, when you do acquire, the
absorption phase of integration is itself a kind of reverse engineering
--- decoding the target's know-how into your own organisation. The
capacity to absorb is the same muscle whether you are learning from a
patent or from a company you bought; it is the difference between owning
the engine and merely owning the machine.

- Should we license, pilot, co-develop, invest, acquire, or sponsor
  research?

- What is the first 90-day experiment?

6\. The Patent-to-Enterprise Exchange

A curated industry--university translation room where senior leaders
explore patents, prototypes and research capabilities and convert
selected inventions into licensing, pilot, joint-development,
investment, acquisition or venture pathways. It is positioned as an
industry strategy exercise, not an academic exhibition.

**Coverage:** anchored by IIT Roorkee, and built to add other institutes
and labs (Kanpur, Kharagpur, Delhi, Bombay, IISc, CSIR and others) as
the network grows. That breadth is the point --- a cohort spanning
pharma, manufacturing and electronics each finds patents in its own
domain rather than everyone staring at the same ten.

> **Positioning line:** *"We don't showcase patents. We run a
